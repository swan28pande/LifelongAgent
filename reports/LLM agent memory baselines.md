# Benchmark against Mem0, Zep, A-MEM and MemoryOS first

The baseline set reviewers expect in late 2026 is clear. It has two controls, **full-context** and **naive RAG**, plus four named systems. **Mem0** (ECAI 2025) is used as a baseline by 12 of 15 recent memory papers. **Zep/Graphiti** (arXiv, 10/15) and **A-MEM** (NeurIPS 2025, 10/15) come next, and **MemoryOS** (EMNLP 2025) is the peer-reviewed hierarchical-memory reference. Your design stores dated facts and preferences in SQL, builds calendar summaries, and retrieves with an agent. For that design, three further baselines carry most of the novelty argument, and each maps onto one of your three components. **TiMem** (Findings of ACL 2026) is a temporal memory tree, the nearest rival to your weekly→monthly→yearly→lifetime hierarchy. **MemGPT/Letta** is the canonical tool-calling memory agent. **LightMem** (ICLR 2026) is the current peer-reviewed efficiency reference. Several papers must be cited but cannot practically be run, because they have no usable code or need RL training on open weights. These are **TReMu** (dated timeline memory with code-executed date arithmetic, Findings of ACL 2025), **ChatDB** (SQL as LLM memory, 2023), **Memory-R1** (learned ADD/UPDATE/DELETE, ACL 2026), **MemForest**, **APEX-MEM** and **TSM**. Scores in this field cannot be compared across papers. Mem0's LoCoMo judge score ranges from 43% to 67% in third-party re-runs against a 92.5% vendor claim, and Zep's ranges from 58% to 95%. So every baseline has to be re-run in one harness, with one Gemini backbone, one embedder and one judge. LoCoMo alone will not support a lifelong-memory claim: it has no knowledge-update questions and its conversations fit in context. Add **LongMemEval-S** (knowledge-update and temporal abilities) and at least one preference-evolution benchmark, ideally **HorizonBench** or **PersonaMem**. **BEAM** is optional for scale.

## Mem0, Zep and A-MEM became the default comparison set in 2025

The standard baseline set changed quickly. Papers from early 2025 (A-MEM, MemoryOS) still compared against the 2023-24 systems from the LoCoMo paper: MemoryBank, MemGPT, ReadAgent and Think-in-Memory. After Mem0 appeared in April 2025, the norm became **Mem0 + Zep + LangMem + A-MEM + full-context + RAG**. Papers from 2026 add efficiency-focused systems (LightMem, Nemori, SimpleMem, MemoryOS) and, increasingly, vendor systems (Memobase, Supermemory, Hindsight, EverMemOS).

A tally over 16 papers from 2025-26 found how often each system was used as a baseline. **Mem0 in 12 papers, Zep in 10, A-MEM in 10, full-context in 9, and LangMem and MemGPT/Letta in 7 each.** MemoryOS, LightMem and Nemori appear in 4 each. The papers counted were LightMem, Memory-R1, MIRIX, SimpleMem, ES-Mem, MemIR, RippleMem, REALM, Hindsight and MemoryAgentBench, among others. A paper was not counted as a baseline for itself. ([Mem0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.19413), [LightMem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18866), [ES-Mem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.07582), [MemIR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25869), [REALM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16053))

The sample leans toward preprints, because most 2026 memory-system papers are still arXiv-only. That gives peer-reviewed systems extra weight when a reviewer asks for "published baselines". The peer-reviewed options are **Mem0 (ECAI 2025, FAIA vol. 413)** ([IOS Press](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/doi/10.3233/FAIA251160)), **A-MEM (NeurIPS 2025)** ([NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/119020)), **MemoryOS (EMNLP 2025 main)** ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1318/)), **LightMem (ICLR 2026)** ([ICLR proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/a05b72653ec5b473732129829ae04195-Abstract-Conference.html)) and **Memory-R1 (ACL 2026)** ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.583/)).

Zep and MemGPT are cited heavily but were never peer-reviewed. Include them anyway, because of how widely they are adopted: Graphiti has about 31k GitHub stars and Letta about 25k ([Graphiti](https://github.com/getzep/graphiti), [Letta](https://github.com/letta-ai/letta)).

The four systems below are the core. Each needs a profile a reviewer would recognize. The results quoted are each paper's own numbers, so they are **not** comparable with one another.

On LongMemEval-S, full-context dropped to 56.80 while A-MEM reached 62.60 ([LightMem PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18866)). That split is itself a finding: **LoCoMo rewards anyone who can fit the conversation in context, and LongMemEval does not.**

## A second tier of 2026 systems covers efficiency, episodic consolidation and profiles

Beyond the core four, five systems are frequent enough, or strong enough, that leaving them out would need a justification.

**LightMem** (ICLR 2026, MIT) is modelled on human memory in three stages. A sensory stage compresses input with LLMLingua-2 and segments it by topic. A short-term buffer summarizes when it fills. Long-term memory is consolidated offline during "sleep" periods. The paper reports LongMemEval-S accuracy **+2.09 to 6.40%** over baselines with **10-38× fewer tokens** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18866v4)). Its repo takes a configurable `BASE_URL` but needs a local LLMLingua-2 model ([GitHub](https://github.com/zjunlp/LightMem)).

**Nemori** (arXiv-only; retitled in v4 as "What Deserves Memory") segments the conversation stream into narrative episodes. It then uses a "predict-calibrate" step to distil only the facts that existing memory fails to predict. It scores LoCoMo J **80.8 with gpt-4.1-mini, against 80.6 for full-context** ([arXiv v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03341v4)). The code is MIT and its README uses OpenRouter with Gemini embeddings ([GitHub](https://github.com/nemori-ai/nemori)).

**EverMemOS** (now EverOS; arXiv, Apache-2.0, about 13k stars) turns dialogue into MemCells: episodic traces, atomic facts and time-bounded "Foresight" signals. These are consolidated into MemScenes, the user profile is updated, and retrieval is agentic. It reports LoCoMo **86.76 (GPT-4o-mini)** and LongMemEval **83.00** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02163)). It runs locally on SQLite + LanceDB with only an OpenRouter key ([GitHub](https://github.com/EverMind-AI/EverMemOS)). Of all the 2026 systems, its profile-updating design is the closest in spirit to your preference table.

**SimpleMem** (arXiv-only) is already a baseline in MemIR and RippleMem. With gpt-4.1-mini it reports LongMemEval **76.87** against Mem0 at 59.81 and LightMem at 68.67 ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.02553)).

**LangMem** (MIT, no paper) is cheap to run and appears in 7 papers ([docs](https://langchain-ai.github.io/langmem/)). Its scores are very unstable, though: 37.20 to 50.80 on LongMemEval depending on backbone, and 58.10 to 78.05 on LoCoMo depending on the paper ([LightMem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18866), [MIRIX](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07957)).

**MemGPT/Letta** needs separate treatment. It is arXiv-only and dates from 2023. In most papers it appears only as a LoCoMo F1 row copied from A-MEM, and Zep reports that it could not easily be evaluated on LongMemEval ([Zep PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.13956)). Even so, it is the canonical **agentic, tool-calling memory** baseline: the LLM pages data between main context and archival/recall stores through function calls ([MemGPT PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.08560)). Letta supports Gemini and Vertex natively ([Letta docs](https://docs.letta.com/guides/server/providers/google)).

Letta's own experiment makes a point your paper has to address. An agent that simply stores conversation history in files, using gpt-4o-mini, scored **74.0% on LoCoMo, above Mem0^g's 68.5%** ([Letta blog](https://www.letta.com/blog/benchmarking-ai-agent-memory/)). An agentic retriever therefore has to beat a trivial agentic retriever, not just Mem0. The filesystem agent is the cheapest strong control available.

The **RL-trained memory managers** are important to cite but hard to run with Gemini. **Memory-R1** (ACL 2026) trains a Memory Manager to choose ADD/UPDATE/DELETE/NOOP and an Answer Agent to filter 60 retrieved candidates, using only 152 QA pairs. On LoCoMo with LLaMA-3.1-8B it reports **+28% relative F1 over MemoryOS** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19828v5)). **Mem-α** trains a Qwen3-4B agent to maintain core, semantic and timestamped episodic memory ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25911v1)). Both need local GPU training on open-weight models ([Memory-R1 repo](https://github.com/yansikuan/memory-r1), [Mem-alpha repo](https://github.com/wangyu-ustc/Mem-alpha)), so Gemini could serve only as a frozen component. Memory-R1 is the peer-reviewed reference for learned overwrite-versus-append decisions, which your SQL supersession logic makes by rule or by prompt. Cite it and discuss it; run it only if you have the GPUs.

## Your novelty sits between TiMem, TReMu, Graphiti and ChatDB

No 2025-26 peer-reviewed conversational-memory paper found in this research combines a relational SQL store of dated facts, preferences and events with **calendar-aligned** hierarchical summaries and an agentic retriever. That is the gap. The search was broad but not exhaustive, so phrase the claim as "to our knowledge". Each component of your design has a close precedent that must be cited and contrasted.

For the **time hierarchy**, the closest rival is **TiMem** ("Temporal-Hierarchical Memory Consolidation for Long-Horizon Conversational Agents", Findings of ACL 2026 per arXiv metadata). It builds a Temporal Memory Tree running from raw observations up to abstracted persona representations, and it makes time the primary organizing principle. It reports **LoCoMo 75.30% and LongMemEval-S 76.88%** with a 52.20% reduction in memory length ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02845)). Code exists at github.com/TiMEM-AI/timem, though the license is unstated. Whether its levels are calendar-aligned (day/week/month) is **not known from the abstract**, so read the PDF before writing your novelty paragraph.

**MemForest** (arXiv 2605.23986, a VLDB submission) treats memory as a write-efficient temporal data-management problem. It uses a time-ordered tree index with localized "dirty-path" refreshes and reports LongMemEval-S **81.8%** and LoCoMo **84.09%** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23986)). It has no code. Its framing as a database problem makes it the paper reviewers will most readily set against a SQL design.

The older hierarchy references are **MemTree** (ICLR 2025), a semantic rather than temporal tree ([ICLR proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/0382cb76309820f71c6eacd47b36ce71-Abstract-Conference.html)), **RAPTOR** (recursive clustering and summarization) and **RSum** (recursive summarization, Neurocomputing 2025) ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231225008653)). MemoryOS's tiers are driven by recency and heat, not by the calendar, and that contrast should be stated explicitly.

For **dated events and temporal arithmetic**, the key precedent is **TReMu** (Findings of ACL 2025). It summarizes each session into events with inferred dates and has the LLM write Python for date calculations, lifting GPT-4o from **29.83 to 77.67** on its multi-session temporal QA benchmark ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.972/)). SQL date predicates are a structured generalization of that idea. Frame them that way, and consider TReMu's benchmark as an extra evaluation.

Three more timeline-oriented papers deserve a paragraph of related work. **THEANINE** (NAACL 2025) never deletes memories and links them into temporal and cause-effect timelines ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.435/)). **TSM** (Findings of ACL 2026) indexes memories by when an event *occurred* rather than by dialogue time ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1496/)). **APEX-MEM** (ACL 2026) combines an append-only temporal property graph with a multi-tool retrieval agent that resolves changing facts at query time, reporting **88.88% on LoCoMo** ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.749/)). APEX-MEM is the closest published match to "structured store + agentic tool-calling + temporal validity", but no code link was found.

For **temporal validity**, Zep/Graphiti's bi-temporal edges are the canonical comparison for your valid-from/superseded rows. There is a counterpoint: a 2026 third-party evaluation reportedly found Graphiti scoring only **7.0% on conflict-resolution tasks** despite strong LongMemEval results. The source paper (possibly MemConflict, arXiv 2605.20926) was not confirmed, so verify it before citing.

For **SQL as memory**, the only direct precedent is **ChatDB** (arXiv 2023, no license). There the LLM writes SQL to read and write a relational database as symbolic memory ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.03901)). ChatDB was never evaluated on LoCoMo or LongMemEval, which leaves room for your paper to be the first to test SQL memory on modern benchmarks. A ChatDB-style "SQL only, no hierarchy, no agent loop" variant should be one of your **ablations**, not an external baseline.

Two more papers are worth citing on the preference side. **RMM** (ACL 2025, Google) builds topic memories at several granularities and trains an online reranker. It was evaluated with Gemini-1.5-Flash, but it has no public code ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.413/)). **SeCom** (ICLR 2025) uses topical segments with compression ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=xKDZAW0He3)). **MemoryBank** (AAAI 2024) is the classic reference for time-based forgetting (Ebbinghaus decay) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10250)).

If TiMem's code cannot be adapted in time, reimplement its tree from the paper and label it as a reimplementation. **LangMem is left out of the top eight** because its scores swing by about 20 points between papers. It is still cheap to add if a reviewer asks.

## Add LongMemEval and a preference-drift benchmark to LoCoMo

LoCoMo alone cannot carry a lifelong-memory claim. Its conversations are **16k-26k tokens and fit in context**, it has **no knowledge-update questions**, and an independent audit found **99 of 1,540 gold answers (6.4%) wrong**, which caps realistic accuracy at about 93.6% ([Zep blog](https://blog.getzep.com/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/), [Penfield audit](https://penfieldlabs.substack.com/p/we-audited-locomo-64-of-the-answer)). The audit is a blog post, not peer-reviewed. Top 2026 scores (APEX-MEM 88.88, EverMemOS 93.05 with gpt-4.1-mini) sit close to that ceiling.

**LongMemEval-S** (ICLR 2025) is the required second benchmark. It has 500 questions over histories of about 115k tokens, and it scores the knowledge-update, temporal-reasoning and abstention abilities separately ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.10813)). There, full-context falls to 55-60% and memory design actually matters ([Zep PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.13956)). Report its knowledge-update and temporal columns prominently.

To back the preference-regime-shift claim with an external benchmark alongside your synthetic set, add **HorizonBench** (arXiv 2026). It has 360 simulated users with 6-month histories (about 163k tokens) generated from mental-state graphs, so the preference shifts have ground truth. The best model scores **52.8%**, and models pick the originally stated value instead of the update "over a third of the time" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17283)). That is exactly the failure a dated, superseding preference table should fix.

**PersonaMem** (COLM 2025) is the peer-reviewed alternative. It has 180+ users and up to 60 sessions, and frontier models reach about 50% on its evolving-profile questions ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14225)).

**BEAM** (ICLR 2026) scales to 10M-token conversations and scores contradiction resolution and knowledge update separately ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.27246)). Add it if you want a horizon claim beyond 2 years. MemIR's BEAM-100K table already gives reference points: Mem0 37.05, Nemori 40.45, SimpleMem 40.68 ([MemIR PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25869)).

Three benchmarks are optional. **MemoryAgentBench** (ICLR 2026) tests selective forgetting through FactConsolidation ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.05257)). **PrefEval** (ICLR 2025) tests whether a stated preference is followed, not whether it changes ([ICLR](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/oral/31848)). **TReMu's temporal QA set** tests date arithmetic.

**LoCoMo-Plus** adds "cognitive" questions but keeps the original 1,540 questions unchanged, including the audited errors ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.10715)).

Newer arXiv suites on memory invalidation (STALE, Supersede, MemOps) are too new to be standard. They are worth a sentence as evidence that update handling is still unsolved.

## Fair baselines require one harness, one judge, and native timestamps

The scoring disputes are specific enough to set rules.

In May 2025 Zep claimed 84% on LoCoMo. Mem0's CTO showed that the figure counted adversarial category-5 answers in the numerator but not the denominator, and corrected it to **58.44%** ([zep-papers#5](https://github.com/getzep/zep-papers/issues/5)). Zep replied that Mem0's own evaluation of Zep had been misconfigured in three ways: the user role was assigned to both speakers, timestamps were appended to the message text instead of set in `created_at`, and searches ran sequentially. Zep's re-run gave **75.14 ± 0.17 J** ([Zep blog](https://blog.getzep.com/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/)).

Two more pitfalls are documented. The A-MEM and Mem0 lines of work **permute the LoCoMo category names**: the same A-MEM row is labelled "multi-hop 27.02" in one and "single-hop 27.02" in the other ([A-MEM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12110), [Mem0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.19413)). And MemoryOS's re-run of A-MEM gave temporal F1 **8.04, against A-MEM's own 45.85** ([MemoryOS PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1318.pdf)). Finally, the standard gpt-4o-mini judge accepted **62.81% of deliberately wrong but on-topic answers** in the Penfield audit ([audit repo](https://github.com/dial481/locomo-audit)).

The protocol follows from these failures. Use a **single Gemini model** for both memory construction and answering in every system, a **single embedding model**, and a **single judge with its prompt published**. Mem0's own guide concedes that differences in judge, answer model and reranking alone keep two labs from matching ([Mem0 guide](https://mem0.ai/blog/ai-memory-benchmarks-in-2026)).

Re-run every baseline and copy no numbers, following the MIRIX and LightMem precedent ([MIRIX PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07957)). Use each system's official ingestion API, with correct speaker roles and **native timestamp fields**. This matters more for your dataset than for LoCoMo, because the whole 2-year preference-shift benchmark depends on dates. Where a system has no timestamp field, prefix every message with its date in the same format for all such systems, and document it.

For LoCoMo, **exclude category 5**, publish the mapping from category ID to name, and report J, F1 and BLEU-1. That keeps you comparable with both the A-MEM/MemoryOS line (F1) and the Mem0 line (J). Also report results on the audit-corrected question subset.

Run each configuration **three times** and report the standard deviation. Add a second judge from a different model family, or a small human-verified subset, to address judge leniency. When you use a Gemini judge on Gemini answers, a self-preference concern arises; this is an inference, not a documented finding.

Report **ingestion cost** (LLM calls and tokens per stored day) and **query cost** (tokens, calls, p95 latency) next to accuracy. Run searches in parallel where the vendor supports it. MemoryOS, A-MEM and Mem0 differ by more than 5× in tokens per response ([MemoryOS PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1318.pdf)), and your agentic retriever will be judged on the same axis.

Where a paper's backbone is OpenAI, note that Mem0 and LangMem drop sharply on open-weight backbones. Mem0 fell to 43.31 on LoCoMo with Qwen3-30B ([LightMem PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18866)). Gemini re-runs will differ from the published numbers, and that is expected rather than a sign of misconfiguration.

## Conclusion

The field has converged on **benchmarks** faster than on **methods**. Almost every paper reports LoCoMo, yet the ranking of the incumbent systems changes with the backbone and the judge. The most defensible contribution for your paper is therefore not another LoCoMo leaderboard number. It is a controlled demonstration, on data with dated regime shifts, that structured supersession and calendar-aligned summaries beat both learned-extraction stores (Mem0, A-MEM) and validity-interval graphs (Graphiti) on knowledge-update and preference-drift questions, at a comparable token budget. LoCoMo then serves as a sanity check rather than the headline.

Two risks stand out. First, TiMem and MemForest already organize memory by time, so your hierarchy's novelty rests on calendar alignment plus SQL plus agentic tool use together, not on temporal hierarchy alone. Confirm TiMem's level structure before claiming otherwise. Second, a Letta-style filesystem agent may come uncomfortably close to your result on LoCoMo. That would support Zep's and Letta's critique of the benchmark, and it is further reason to lead with LongMemEval knowledge-update, HorizonBench-style preference drift, and your own 2-year set.
